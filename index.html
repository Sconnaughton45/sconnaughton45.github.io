Case study: one weekly forecast for the whole company

The problem

Inventory, call-center staffing, marketing and finance each planned from their own view of the week, so decisions drifted apart.

What I built

A weekly sales and forecast book, built from scratch and refreshed every week. It compared actual sales to budget, last year and two years back by business and channel. For the weeks ahead, I projected the active catalogs, updated circulation, response rate and average order value for each mailing, then adjusted the weekly sales flow.

What it did

One outlook, shared across the company, used for inventory planning, call-center staffing, marketing investment and projected profitability.

Case study: what a new customer is worth a year later

The problem

Marketing decisions were being made without a clear view of what a new customer was worth after the first order.

What I built

A one-year customer value analysis that joined internal sales and cost data from Oracle Hyperion with web metrics from Google Analytics. For each group of new customers, it tracked what they spent in the year after their first purchase, then subtracted catalog, shipping, order-handling and web advertising costs to reach contribution per customer. I cut it by first-order size, where the customer came from, first channel, month, refunds and free shipping.

What it did

It gave the company its first real look at customer value after the first order, and a summary slide for the board. The findings shaped how acquisition spend was allocated across channels.

A simplified rebuild with invented numbers. Switch the view, then change how many catalogs are mailed to see what it does to value.

Value is measured in the year after the first order. Contribution is sales after cancellations and refunds, times gross margin, plus shipping revenue, minus shipping, order-handling, catalog and web advertising costs. The first order itself is not counted.

Case study: is the next catalog worth mailing?

The problem

Every extra catalog costs real money, and it was hard to tell whether it created new orders or only collected orders that would have come anyway.

What I built

A controlled holdout test. I randomly split customers into two equal groups over the same stretch of weeks. One group got the standard schedule and the other got one extra catalog. I compared orders, demand, cancellations, refunds, gross margin and catalog cost, then cut the results by how recently each customer had bought and how often.

What it did

The test showed where the extra catalog paid for itself and where it didn’t, so mailings could be cut where they lost money. Across the program, holdout testing helped cut circulation by 50% and raise profit per contact by more than 150%.

Added contribution is extra sales after cancellations and refunds, times gross margin, minus the cost of the extra catalog. It assumes 85% of demand survives cancellations and refunds and a 70% gross margin. Break-even lift is the sales increase the extra catalog needs just to cover its own cost.

More work I’ve built

Which products earn their page space?

A weekly selling-by-style report, built from scratch. It combined Hyperion sales and cost data with an offline file listing every style, the page it ran on and the space it took. Styles were ranked several ways, including units, demand and demand per page, and each catalog was compared to plan and to last year. Every active catalog was projected to completion, and the whole report was refreshed each Monday.

It drove inventory forecasts, evaluations of rates such as gross margin and refund percentage, and decisions about page space.

Which emails and texts actually make money?

A tracking system for every email and text the company sent. It brought together send and engagement data from the email platform, internal order and demand reporting, Google Analytics web metrics and SMS results. Campaign codes carried the brand, week, send date and offer, so every send could be sorted and compared. Reports ranked sends by profit per 1,000 emails, not just opens and clicks, and tracked trends by week and month, by send time, and by active and inactive audiences, with comparisons to last week and last year.

It gave the team one place to see what each offer and message earned.

Which web spend is working?

A weekly web spend dashboard I built for the monthly review with the CEO. For each brand, it tracked spend against budget and last year across brand, non-brand and shopping search, affiliates and remarketing. Revenue was credited using both first-click and last-click attribution, then averaged. Each channel’s advertising-to-sales ratio was rated profitable, break-even or losing, so the discussion could focus on what to cut, hold or scale.

It covered the online ad budget and was the basis for monthly decisions on where that money should go, including cutting paid search spend by 30% while demand held flat.

One report for the whole company

A company-wide master report I built from the ground up as the company’s centralized reporting source, used by accounting and every department. It put sales, orders, demand, cancellations, refunds, free-shipping rates and gross margin in one place, cut by week, month, quarter and year on the fiscal calendar, with actuals alongside budget and forecast. It broke results out by brand, channel (phone, web and mail), customer group and marketing code, and carried an end-of-month dashboard and catalog comparisons against last year.

Weekly, monthly and year-end reports all ran off it, so accounting and the rest of the company worked from one shared set of numbers on the same fiscal timing.
